2024 · Product-led growth

Expanding Orca Scan into the design ecosystem

A side bet that paid: designing and shipping a Figma plugin from scratch opened an acquisition channel into an audience the product had never reached.

Role
Design and code, end to end
Stack
Figma Plugin API · JavaScript · HTML · CSS
Outcome
950+ users in the Figma Community

The opportunity

Orca Scan helps enterprise teams across manufacturing, healthcare and logistics manage barcode data at scale. Our users are operations teams. But a different kind of user kept turning up: designers building packaging, signage and event material in Figma, who needed barcodes as part of their workflow.

They weren't our core user. They were touching our ecosystem anyway.

Talking it through with our founder, we found the gap. Figma had no good barcode generator. The plugins that existed were outdated, unreliable, or painful to use. That was an opening — a tool that solved a real problem for a new audience and put Orca Scan in front of the design community at the same time.

I took ownership of it.

What I owned

This was a full product initiative, not a design handoff:

  • Competitive research across the existing plugins — 20+ analysed
  • Problem definition and scope
  • UX and UI, built on Orca Scan's design system
  • Front-end implementation — vanilla JS, HTML, CSS, Figma Plugin API
  • Integration with our barcode generation logic (QR, EAN, Code 128)
  • Testing, iteration and the Community launch

Making the case before making the thing

Before opening Figma I had to answer whether this was worth building at all. Three angles:

User need. Designers need barcodes for print, packaging and events regularly. The workaround — generate somewhere else, export, re-import — was slow and error-prone. A native plugin removed that friction entirely.

Business case. Every designer who installs the plugin is a potential Orca Scan lead. Low-cost presence in a community we weren't reaching at all.

Feasibility. The Plugin API is well documented and our barcode logic already existed, so the integration was tractable. The real unknown was my own JavaScript — which I treated as a constraint to work around, not a reason to stop.

Designing for a panel

I designed the UI in Figma before writing a line of code, pulling tokens from our design system so the plugin felt like the product even at this scale.

The constraint was specific. Figma panels are small, modal, and users have no patience for complexity inside them. Every element had to earn its place. I cut three features from the initial scope because they added friction without adding value.

Building it

I had never shipped a Figma plugin. I approached it the way I approach a design problem: research the constraints, break it into parts, start small.

The architecture needed four files working together — manifest.json, ui.html, code.js and style.css. I learned the Plugin API by building against it rather than waiting until I felt ready.

The hardest part wasn't the code. It was knowing what not to build.

Scoping a v1 that was small enough to ship and useful enough to matter, instead of a bloated one that never launched.

Results

950+ users in the Figma Community since launch
New audience reached — designers, outside our core market
$0 marketing spend — the distribution was the design

It shipped on Orca Scan's channels and on LinkedIn, and it proved that a design-led product bet can create its own distribution without a budget behind it.

What it changed in how I work

Designing the UI was the easy half. The harder and more valuable skill was making the product case — why this, why now, what success looks like — and then building the thing myself, because waiting for engineering capacity would have meant it never existed.

The distance between having the idea and having 950 users is execution. That's what I learned to close.
