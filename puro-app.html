Puro App — from UX student to Product Designer

A wellness app concept I designed during my first UX/UI course. Five years later, I revisited it with a product mindset — critiquing my past decisions and reframing the problem.

Why revisit a student project?

Most designers quietly delete their first projects. I decided to do the opposite: publish mine, then publicly review it with everything I've learned since.

Puro was a wellness app concept I designed in 2020 during my first UX/UI course. It had everything a student project has: enthusiasm, a polished UI, a Figma file full of screens — and almost no product thinking.

The most valuable design skill I've built in five years isn't visual. It's knowing which questions to ask before opening Figma.

What 2020-me did

I followed the process I was taught: a survey, some personas, a user journey, wireframes, and a high-fidelity UI. The result looked good — calm palette, clean typography, friendly onboarding.

But looking back, the project optimized for deliverables, not decisions. Every artifact existed because the course required it, not because it answered a question I had about the product.

[ Original 2020 screens ]

What 2025-me sees

The problem was never validated. "People want to build healthy habits" is not a problem statement — it's a market category. Who exactly? What have they already tried? Why did it fail? 2020-me never asked.

No business model, no constraints. The concept floated free of any real-world tension: no acquisition cost, no retention target, no competitive pressure. Design without constraints isn't easier — it's directionless.

Features as decoration. The app had streaks, badges, and social sharing because wellness apps have those things. Each feature was a pattern copied, not a hypothesis tested.

The UI hid the gaps. A polished surface made weak thinking look finished. This is the most dangerous student habit — and the one the industry accidentally rewards.

How I'd approach it today

  • Start with the riskiest assumption — that people abandon wellness apps because of friction, not motivation. Test that before designing anything.
  • Define success metrics first — what does retention look like at week 4? What's the one behavior the product must drive?
  • Scope a real MVP — one core loop, shippable, measurable. Not 40 screens.
  • Design the system, not the screens — tokens, components, and states from day one, so iteration is cheap.

What this case study is really about

This isn't a redesign. I deliberately didn't repaint the old screens — that would miss the point.

It's a demonstration of the distance between decorating a problem and solving one. Five years of shipping real products — with real users, real metrics, and real constraints — changed not how I push pixels, but how I decide which pixels deserve to exist.

Every senior designer has a Puro in their past. I'd rather show mine and what it taught me than pretend I arrived fully formed.
